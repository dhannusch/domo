# Craft: identity in code

What separates a professional result from a generated one is editing and detail, not the tool.

## Wordmarks first

- Set the name in 6–8 candidate typefaces that fit the territory, at display size, on one sheet.
  Compare them side by side before choosing; don't commit to the first serif or grotesque you think of.
- Judge letter shapes you'll live with: the a, g, s, the capitals in the name, and how the
  words sit together. A name like "How Teams Ship" lives or dies on the H, T and S.
- Tune spacing by eye, pair by pair: default tracking is set for running text, not logos. Tighten
  large display settings; open small ones. Check the gaps between words too.
- Earn one deliberate detail at most (a custom letter, a cut, a ligature, a joined pair). Many
  details make it look busy; none makes it look like a font sample.
- Check case and weight: sentence case, all caps and small caps say different things; a heavier
  weight survives small sizes better.

## Marks

- Only make a symbol when the brand needs a small square presence (avatar, favicon) the wordmark
  can't fill. Often the answer is a monogram or a cropped letter from the wordmark.
- Draw on a grid: a 48 or 96 unit `viewBox`, integer coordinates, consistent stroke widths and
  corner radii. Few paths; no filters or gradients.
- Correct optically: round shapes overshoot the grid slightly, horizontals are a touch thinner than
  verticals, and a visually centered shape sits a little above true center.
- Prefer one simple, ownable silhouette over a picture of the idea. Fill it solid black: if the
  silhouette isn't recognizable, it isn't a mark yet.
- Design the 16px version as its own drawing if the large one muddies.

## Type system

- Two families at most (often one): a display face for headlines and a text face for reading.
- Set a real scale (e.g. 1.25 or 1.333) and line heights per size; test headlines that wrap.

## Color

- Start from the ground and ink, not the accent. Most strong publications are mostly neutral with
  one accent used sparingly.
- Give each color a role (ground, ink, accent, support) and measure contrast for each text pair.
- Derive color from the territory and its feeling, not from the industry ("tech" isn't a color).

## Applications

- Show work in context from stage 2 on: a newsletter cover, a profile picture, an email header, at
  their real pixel sizes. A mark that looks good alone and weak in use isn't done.
- Covers should get their impact from type and space. Decoration that explains the article belongs
  in the article.

## Critique

Critique what's on screen, specifically: "the gap between w and s is wider than t and e", "the
accent disappears at 32px", "this reads as a consulting firm because of the italic serif and rust".
Generic praise or a score is not a critique. Fix the top two or three problems, then look again.
